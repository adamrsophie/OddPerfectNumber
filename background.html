Background and glossary

The terms used across this site, each with a precise definition, a small example, and a link to where the library uses it. Every example on this page was recomputed with exact integer arithmetic before publication.

Notation: N is a hypothetical odd perfect number, p, q, r are primes, and a | b means that a divides b. Lean writes the divisor sum of n as σ 1 n and the exponent of p in n as n.factorization p or padicValNat p n.

Glossary: divisor sum · perfect, abundant, deficient · abundancy · component · p-adic valuation · multiplicative order · cyclotomic factorization · lifting the exponent · primitive prime divisor · Euler's form and the special prime · ordinary primes · ω and Ω · Legendre and Jacobi symbols · spoof factorization

Divisor sums

Divisor sum σ

Definition. σ(n) is the sum of all positive divisors of n, including 1 and n. It is multiplicative: σ(ab) = σ(a)σ(b) whenever a and b are coprime. For a prime power, σ(pa) = 1 + p + p² + ⋯ + pa.

Example. σ(12) = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 6 + 12 = 28. With 45 = 3²·5: σ(45) = σ(3²)σ(5) = 13·6 = 78.

In the library. mathlib's ArithmeticFunction.sigma; the multiplicativity is used through isMultiplicative_sigma in almost every module, starting with Seed.Starter.

Perfect, abundant and deficient numbers

Definition. n is perfect when σ(n) = 2n, abundant when σ(n) > 2n, and deficient when σ(n) < 2n. Every multiple of an abundant number is abundant, so a perfect number has no abundant divisor. This is the most used closing argument in the library.

Example. 6 and 28 are perfect: σ(6) = 12 and σ(28) = 56. 12 is abundant (28 > 24) and 8 is deficient (15 < 16). The smallest odd abundant number is 945, with σ(945) = 1920 > 1890.

In the library. Nat.Perfect from mathlib; not_perfect_of_dvd_of_two_lt_abundancy.

Abundancy

Definition. The abundancy of n is the rational number σ(n)/n. It is multiplicative on coprime arguments, n is perfect exactly when its abundancy is 2, and for a prime power it is a geometric sum in 1/p that stays strictly below p/(p − 1). Hence the abundancy of n is below the product of p/(p − 1) over its prime factors, and a perfect N needs that product to exceed 2.

Example. abundancy(45) = 78/45 = 26/15, which equals abundancy(9)·abundancy(5) = (13/9)(6/5). abundancy(945) = 1920/945 = 128/63, a little above 2.

In the library. Abundancy defines abundancy n as an exact rational; two_lt_prod_primeFactors is the product bound.

Prime power component and exact exponent

Definition. For a prime p dividing N, the exact exponent is the largest e with pe dividing N, and pe is the p component of N. Because σ is multiplicative, σ(N) is the product of the component sums σ(pe), and each σ(pe) divides σ(N) = 2N. So every odd prime dividing σ(pe) divides N: the forcing step. The exponent must be exact: σ(3²) = 13 but σ(3⁴) = 121 = 11².

Example. If 3 divides an odd perfect number exactly to the power 2, then σ(3²) = 13 divides 2N, so 13 divides N.

In the library. OPNStarter.force_prime and thirteen_dvd_of_three_sq.

Valuations and orders

p-adic valuation

Definition. vp(n) is the exponent of the prime p in n, so vp(ab) = vp(a) + vp(b). Taking vq of σ(N) = 2N gives one linear equation per prime q: the exponents of q in the component sums add up to vq(N), plus 1 when q = 2. These equations are the ledgers of the Methods page.

Example. σ(13²) = 183 = 3·61, so v₃(σ(13²)) = 1. σ(5) = 6 has v₂ = 1, while σ(3) = 4 has v₂ = 2.

In the library. OPNStarter.valuation_balance; exact formulas in Valuation.

Multiplicative order

Definition. For a prime q not dividing a, the order of a modulo q is the least d ≥ 1 with ad ≡ 1 (mod q). It divides q − 1, the number of nonzero residues modulo q, and an ≡ 1 (mod q) exactly when d divides n.

Example. 3³ = 27 = 2·13 + 1, so 3 has order 3 modulo 13, and 3 divides 13 − 1 = 12. 3⁵ = 243 = 22·11 + 1, so 3 has order 5 modulo 11.

In the library. mathlib's orderOf (p : ZMod q); see prime_dvd_sigma_prime_pow.

Cyclotomic factorization of σ(pa)

Definition. σ(pa) = (pa+1 − 1)/(p − 1), and xn − 1 is the product of the cyclotomic polynomials Φd(x) over the divisors d of n. Hence σ(pa) is the product of Φd(p) over the divisors d > 1 of a + 1. The order dichotomy follows: a prime q dividing σ(pa) is not p, and either p ≡ 1 (mod q) with q dividing a + 1, or the order of p modulo q is greater than 1 and divides both a + 1 and q − 1.

Example. σ(3⁵) = (3⁶ − 1)/2 = 364, and Φ₂(3)Φ₃(3)Φ₆(3) = (3 + 1)(9 + 3 + 1)(9 − 3 + 1) = 4·13·7 = 364. The prime 13 is a divisor of Φ₃(3), and 3 has order 3 modulo 13.

In the library. sigma_prime_pow_eq_prod_cyclotomic in Cyclotomic.

Lifting the exponent

Definition. For an odd prime q dividing x − y (and not dividing xy), vq(xn − yn) = vq(x − y) + vq(n). Applied to σ(pa)(p − 1) = pa+1 − 1 with q dividing p − 1, it gives vq(σ(pa)) = vq(a + 1).

Example. p = 7 and q = 3 (3 divides 6). σ(7²) = 57 = 3·19 has v₃ = 1 = v₃(3); σ(7⁵) has v₃ = v₃(6) = 1; σ(7⁸) has v₃ = v₃(9) = 2.

In the library. padicValNat_sigma_prime_pow_of_dvd_pred, built on mathlib's padicValNat.pow_sub_pow.

Primitive prime divisor

Definition. A prime q is a primitive prime divisor of an − 1 when a has order exactly n modulo q: q divides an − 1 but no ak − 1 with k < n. Then n divides q − 1. The library proves that for every base a ≥ 3 and every n ≥ 3 such a prime exists. If p has exact exponent e in N and d ≥ 3 divides e + 1, the primitive prime of order d divides σ(pd−1), which divides σ(pe), so it is a prime factor of N that is ≡ 1 (mod d).

Example. 3⁴ − 1 = 80 = 2⁴·5, and 5 divides none of 3 − 1, 3² − 1, 3³ − 1 (that is 2, 8, 26), so 5 is primitive for n = 4, and 4 divides 5 − 1.

In the library. exists_prime_orderOf_eq and exists_primeFactor_modEq_one in Zsigmondy.

The shape of an odd perfect number

Euler's form and the special prime

Definition. Every odd perfect number can be written N = qkm² with q prime, q not dividing m, and q ≡ k ≡ 1 (mod 4). The prime q is the special prime: it is the only prime with an odd exponent. The reason is the 2-adic ledger: σ(N) = 2N has exactly one factor 2, and for odd p the sum σ(pe) of e + 1 odd terms is even exactly when e is odd.

Example. The pair (q, k) = (5, 1) is allowed: σ(5) = 6 has exactly one factor 2. The pair (3, 1) is not: σ(3) = 4 has two. Neither is (5, 3): σ(5³) = 156 = 4·39.

In the library. exists_euler_form and exists_special_prime in Euler.

Ordinary primes

Definition. The prime factors of N other than the special prime. Their exponents are even, hence at least 2. Exponent patterns such as "all ordinary exponents equal 2" are statements about these primes.

Example. 7 ≡ 3 (mod 4), so 7 can never be the special prime: if 7 divides N, then 7² divides N.

In the library. sq_dvd_of_prime_dvd_of_mod_four_eq_three, even_factorization_of_ne.

ω and Ω

Definition. ω(N) is the number of distinct prime factors and Ω(N) the number of prime factors counted with multiplicity, that is the sum of the exponents. Since every ordinary exponent is at least 2, 2ω ≤ Ω + 1 for an odd perfect number.

Example. 4455 = 3⁴·5·11 has ω = 3 and Ω = 6.

In the library. ω N and Ω N from mathlib, or N.primeFactors.card; see Counting.

Quadratic residues

Legendre and Jacobi symbols

Definition. For an odd prime p, the Legendre symbol (a/p) is 1 if a is a nonzero square modulo p, −1 if it is not a square, and 0 if p divides a. It is multiplicative in a. The Jacobi symbol (a/n) for odd n is the product of the Legendre symbols over the prime factors of n with multiplicity. Quadratic reciprocity: for distinct odd primes p and r, (p/r)(r/p) = −1 exactly when p ≡ r ≡ 3 (mod 4).

Example. (2/7) = 1 because 3² = 9 ≡ 2 (mod 7), and (3/7) = −1 because the nonzero squares modulo 7 are 1, 2, 4. The Jacobi symbol (2/15) = (2/3)(2/5) = (−1)(−1) = 1, although 2 is not a square modulo 15: for composite n a Jacobi symbol 1 does not mean a square. The library applies jacobiSym only at primes, where it is the Legendre symbol.

In the library. Reciprocity, after Wallace (arXiv 1610.00544).

Countermodels

Spoof factorization

Definition. A list of bases and exponents, treated as if every base were prime: its value is the product of be and its formal divisor sum is the product of 1 + b + ⋯ + be. A spoof odd perfect number has formal divisor sum equal to twice its value, together with the side conditions recorded in IsSpoofOddPerfect (odd bases at least 3, pairwise coprime, positive exponents, Euler's parity pattern). If every base is prime, the spoof is a genuine odd perfect number.

Example. The Descartes spoof 3²·7²·11²·13²·22021 has formal divisor sum 13·57·133·183·22022 = 2·198585576189, twice its value. But 22021 = 19²·61 is not prime: the number 198585576189 has six distinct prime factors, and its true divisor sum 426027470778 is not twice the number, 397171152378.

In the library. Spoof; see also the diagram of its network and Why it is hard.
